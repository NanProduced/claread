European Central Bank Holds Rates Steady as Inflation Eases Across the Eurozone

The European Central Bank kept its main interest rate unchanged on Thursday, signalling that policymakers believe the recent fall in inflation is now durable enough to pause a long cycle of tightening. The decision, which was widely expected by economists, leaves the deposit rate at four percent, where it has stood since the last move in September. The bank repeated language from its previous statement, saying that inflation had "declined substantially" over the past year and that underlying price pressures had started to weaken, while warning that domestic price increases in services and wages still needed close monitoring.

President Christine Lagarde, speaking after the meeting in Frankfurt, told reporters that the governing council had spent most of its two-day debate discussing the pace at which the bank should communicate the end of restrictive policy. She said the council was "not yet at the point" of declaring victory on inflation and would continue to follow a data-dependent approach. Investors had hoped for clearer hints on when the first rate cut might arrive, and eurozone government bond yields edged higher after the press conference as traders scaled back bets on an early move. Several sell-side economists revised their forecasts within hours, pushing back the expected start of cuts from April to June.

Behind the formal statement lies a more complicated picture. Headline inflation in the eurozone dropped to two point four percent last month, only a little above the bank's two percent target, and core inflation, which excludes the most volatile food and energy items, has fallen for six straight months. Wage growth, however, is still running close to five percent, and several measures of services inflation remain stubbornly above four percent. That mix is what makes the council uncomfortable about moving too quickly, according to officials briefed on the discussion. The bank also pointed to a series of one-off effects from changes in regulated prices, which had already begun to fade in the latest data.

The political backdrop is also more crowded than it was a year ago. With parliamentary elections looming in several large member states and a new European Commission due to take office later this year, governments are pressing the bank to show that its tightening has not choked off growth. Manufacturing output has been weak for more than a year, and surveys of business confidence have only just begun to stabilise. Some economists worry that the bank risks keeping policy too tight for too long, especially if the energy shock of the past two winters fades faster than expected. Others, including several hawks inside the council, argue that an early move would risk reigniting the very inflation the bank has spent two years trying to bring under control.

For now, the ECB is choosing patience. Officials inside the bank point out that previous episodes of premature easing in the early two-thousands and again after the global financial crisis led to painful second waves of inflation that required even sharper tightening later. They argue that the cost of waiting a few more meetings to be sure is much smaller than the cost of having to reverse course. Market pricing before the meeting had suggested a first cut as early as April, but after Lagarde's comments traders pushed that expectation back to June or even later. The shift was modest, but it sent a clear signal that the council did not yet feel comfortable opening the door to a cut.

The bank's next set of economic projections, due in March, will be closely watched. If wage growth slows and services inflation continues to ease, the council will have more room to begin cutting. If either number surprises to the upside, the message of patience is likely to be repeated for several more meetings. Officials have also signalled that the bar for the first cut will be higher than the bar for the second, on the view that the very first move is the one that does the most to reshape inflation expectations. Either way, the ECB is signalling that it intends to be the last major central bank to move rather than the first, and that the bar for any change in policy is now firmly set on incoming data rather than on calendar dates.

The reaction outside the bank has been mixed. Governments in southern member states, where growth has been particularly weak, have called for a faster easing cycle. Northern governments, where inflation has been more sticky, have been more cautious. The bank has tried to insulate itself from these pressures by emphasising that its decisions are based purely on the outlook for price stability, not on the political calendar. Whether that distinction will hold in practice is one of the questions that markets and economists will be watching closely over the next few months. For households with mortgages tied to the deposit rate, that means relief, if it comes, will arrive more slowly than many had hoped, and borrowers should plan for at least one more quarter of unchanged conditions before any meaningful change.

Inside the council, the mood after the meeting was described by one official as "calm but unresolved". Several members, particularly those from countries with weaker recent inflation prints, had wanted the statement to acknowledge the possibility of a cut as early as the spring. Others, including the bank's chief economist, argued that any such language would risk unmooring inflation expectations at a delicate moment. The compromise was a statement that kept all of the bank's optionality open while signalling, through its careful repetition of the phrase "data-dependent", that the council is in no hurry to move. The phrasing was almost identical to the one used at the previous meeting, and that, in the end, was the point: a deliberate signal of continuity in a year when the political pressure for change will only grow.
