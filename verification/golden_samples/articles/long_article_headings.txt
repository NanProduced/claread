How a Small Island Grid Stayed Lit Through a Three-Day Storm

A regional test of resilience

When the autumn storm warning was first issued, the operators of the small island grid did what they always do: they checked the spare parts cupboard, called the on-call engineers, and sent a short message to the harbour master asking him to keep the fuel barge on standby. The storm, which had been growing in the Atlantic for almost a week, was expected to bring three days of strong winds, heavy rain, and the kind of salt spray that ages metal in months rather than years. The grid has faced this kind of weather before. What made this storm different was the test it would give to a set of small, low-cost changes that the operators had been quietly putting in place over the past two years. None of the changes on their own would have made headlines, but together they would either prove their worth or reveal the next set of gaps that the engineers would have to close.

Section 1: the basic picture

The grid serves just under nine thousand customers across the island, of whom roughly half are connected through overhead lines that run through exposed coastal land. A single 33 kilovolt undersea cable links the island to the mainland, and three small diesel generators provide backup when the cable is offline. The cable is old, well maintained, and the most common single point of failure in the system. Almost every long outage of the past decade has started with a cable fault, and almost every short outage has been caused by a tree falling across an overhead line. The new measures were designed to change those numbers, if only by a little. They were also designed to be cheap, so that they could be extended to other parts of the system without having to wait for a multi-year capital plan.

Section 2: the new measures

Three changes made most of the difference. First, the operators installed fast-acting switches on the most exposed sections of the overhead line, so that a fault could be isolated in under a minute and power rerouted to the rest of the feeder. Second, they put in place a small battery installation at the harbour end of the grid, sized not to power the island but to keep the control room, the desalination plant, and the local hospital running for the first few hours after a cable trip. Third, they reorganised the on-call rota so that two engineers, rather than one, were available at any time during a warning, with a clear handover window at shift change. Each of these was, on its own, a small change. None of them required a new planning consent, and none of them were visible from the road.

Section 3: how the storm played out

The storm arrived on Thursday evening with winds of just over ninety kilometres an hour at the exposed western end of the island. The undersea cable tripped twice, once on Thursday night and again on Saturday morning, and on both occasions the diesel generators started without issue and the battery installation took over the priority loads within seconds. The fast switches operated four times over the three days, isolating three separate overhead line faults and keeping the rest of the affected feeder live. The longest single outage experienced by any customer was just under three hours, and the cumulative hours lost across the whole island were about a third of what they had been in a comparable storm two years earlier. The control room, which is normally staffed during working hours only, was manned continuously for the duration of the warning.

Section 4: what the operators learned

The operators are careful not to declare the new measures a complete success. The battery installation is too small to cover a long cable outage, and the fast switches are only as good as the tree-cutting programme that keeps the lines clear. Several customers in the most exposed villages still lost power for at least an hour during the storm, and a small number of the underground cables that were not yet on the maintenance schedule gave the operators cause for concern. The engineers are now writing a short report for the regulator, with a recommendation that the same set of measures be extended to the next two feeders scheduled for upgrade. They are also recommending a modest expansion of the battery installation, on the grounds that the existing size is enough to keep priority loads running for a few hours but not enough to cover a worst case cable outage that lasts a full working day.

Section 5: why it matters

For most readers, the small island grid will not look like a global story. It is a system that serves a few thousand customers on a single piece of land, and most of the measures that kept it lit through the storm were, in the end, unglamorous: faster switches, a small battery, and a slightly better rota. But the broader lesson is one that any engineer working on a small or ageing grid will recognise. Resilience rarely comes from a single big investment. It comes from a sequence of modest, well-targeted changes, each of which would be easy to dismiss on its own, and each of which buys a little more time when the next storm arrives. The island operators are the first to admit that they have not solved the underlying problem of an ageing undersea cable. What they have done, over the past two years, is to make sure that the next time the cable fails, fewer of their customers will notice.
